Introduction

This week introduces what physiology is and why structure supports function. We begin at the level of cells and membranes, then widen to tissues, organs, and organ systems. We show how systems coordinate to maintain a stable internal environment for cells, a principle known as homeostasis. With that foundation in place, we zoom back to the membrane to learn how molecules move, because transport underpins every system we will study. Finally, we connect this scientific work with principles of health, safety and environment (HSE), human ethics, and academic integrity that allow us to study the human body in a responsible way.

Overview of human physiology

At its heart, physiology is the study of how living systems function, while anatomy is the study of the physical structures that make these functions possible. You'll quickly see that these two disciplines are inseparable; structure enables function. This fundamental principle applies at every level of organisation: from how individual cells are built to form tissues, how tissues create organs, and ultimately, how organs coordinate in complex systems to maintain the body's stable internal environment. 

The major organ systems

Our bodies are organized into specialized systems that perform specific functions. These systems often influence the activities of other systems. As shown in the figure below, there are six levels of structural organization within the body.

At the most basic level, atoms combine to form molecules. The human body depends on various molecules such as water, proteins, and carbohydrates.

Cells are the smallest building blocks of living beings, are composed of specialized molecules.

Groups of similar cells form tissues, which are categorized into epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural tissues.

Organs are made of at least two types of tissue and perform specific functions.

Organ systems consist of two or more organs working together.

Organ systems overview

Organ System Functions
Nervous system The body's fast-acting control system. It includes the brain, spinal cord, nerves, and sense organs. This system detects changes inside and outside the body, interprets signals, and activates muscles and glands in response.
Endocrine system Works together with the nervous system as a control system, but is more slow-acting. Includes glands that secrete hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones travel to target organs, altering gene expression and cellular activity to control growth, reproduction, and nutrient use. There are seven primary endocrine glands in both males and females. Each gland secretes different hormones that affect the body in various ways.
Muscular system Muscles contract and pull their ends together to move body parts, move fluids through internal organs, maintain posture, and produce body heat.
Skeletal system Consists of bones, ligaments, and cartilages. It provides frameworks, protects softer tissues,        serves as muscle attachments, produces blood cells, and stores minerals.
Cardiovascular system Consists of the heart, arteries, capillaries, veins, and blood. The heart pumps blood, which        carries respiratory gases, nutrients, hormones, and wastes throughout the body.
Respiratory system

Includes the nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and lungs. It moves air in and out of the body and exchanges gases between the blood and the air. Oxygen enters the blood from the lungs, and carbon dioxide leaves the blood and is expelled from the body.
Digestive system Includes the mouth, tongue, teeth, salivary glands, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, small intestine, and large intestine. It breaks down food into absorbable forms, absorbs nutrients into the body, and excretes indigestible food as faeces.
Urinary system Consists of the kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, and urethra. The kidneys remove wastes from blood, maintain water and electrolyte balance, and regulate acid-base balance and blood pressure. Other parts of the system store and excrete urine.
Integumentary system The largest body system, includes the skin and its appendages (e.g., hair and nails). It protects inner body structures, regulates body temperature, and synthesizes vitamin D.
Lymphatic system Closely associated with the cardiovascular system. Includes lymphatic vessels, lymph fluid, lymph nodes, the thymus, and spleen. It transports fluid from tissues back to the bloodstream, carries fatty substances from digestive organs, and removes pathogens to defend against infections.
Reproductive system The male reproductive system includes the testes, scrotum, penis, accessory glands, and ducts, producing and maintaining sperm cells, transferring them to the female reproductive tract, and producing male sex hormones. The female reproductive system includes the ovaries, uterine tubes, uterus, and vagina, producing and maintaining eggs, transporting them, receiving sperm, supporting embryo development, carrying a foetus to term, and producing female sex hormones.

Homeostasis
nil
   

Learning objective

  • Define homeostasis, describe the components of a homeostatic control system (receptor, control centre, effector), and explain how negative and positive feedback loops function.

As you have just seen in the overview of the major organ systems, different organs work together to support the needs of cells. This coordination is captured in the concept of homeostasis, which refers to the dynamic maintenance of a stable internal environment.

Examples of physiological variables that are regulated by more than one organ system include body temperature, blood pressure, blood sugar, water balance, and sodium levels.

Homeostasis is maintained through self-regulating control mechanisms based on feedback loops. These mechanisms involve receptors (or sensors) detecting changes, control centres evaluating changes and communicating with effectors, and effectors taking action to restore balance.

Negative feedback

Most homeostatic mechanisms operate on negative feedback loops, where changes in internal conditions trigger a response that restores the system to its set point, as shown in the figure below. This occurs by reversing or counteracting the original change in internal conditions.

An example is a thermostat regulating temperature.

  • Receptors in the skin detect changes in environmental temperature, receptors in the brain detect changes in body temperature.
  • These receptors send signals to the temperature regulatory/control centre in the brain (the hypothalamus).
  • The temperature regulatory/control centre in the brain send signals to sweat glands, which act effectors by producing sweat on the sufrace of the skin.
  • Evaporation of sweat increases heat loss, thereby helping to restore body temperature to within a normal range.

Figure 3. Negative feedback system. Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

Positive feedback

Positive feedback loops, unlike negative feedback, intensify or amplify a change (rather than reversing it). Examples include blood clotting and uterine contractions during childbirth. Positive feedback mechanisms are usually short-lived and have specific functions.

Molecular movement and membrane transport
nil
   

Learning objective

  • Describe molecular movement, osmosis and the different processes of membrane transport.

Having discussed the processes that occur on the whole-body level to maintain homeostasis, we will briefly review some processes that take place on a cellular level.

Cells exchange materials with their environment through the plasma membrane. The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable barrier, allowing some substances to pass across the membrane, while blocking other substances from moving.

Figure 4. Phosholipid bilayer (also known as the plasma or cell membrane). Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

 

Movement of molecules in and out of the cells across the plasma membrane is driven by differences in the concentration of molecules or electrical charge between the intracellular and extracellular fluid.

Simple diffusion

Diffusion is the movement of molecules from a region of high to a region of low concentration, driven by kinetic energy. Because molecules move 'down' their concentration gradient, this process does not require cellular energy. Therefore, diffusion is a form of passive transport. Molecules diffuse through the plasma membrane if they are small, lipid-soluble, or they are transported by carrier proteins in the membrane.

Figure 5. Simple diffusion across the plasma (cell) membrane. Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion involves specific channel proteins helping to transport ions (e.g., sodium, potassium, and chloride) or carrier proteins transporting larger water-soluble molecules like glucose and amino acids across the membrane down a concentration gradient. Channel proteins are less selective than carrier proteins, and usually mildly discriminate between their cargo based on size and charge. Carrier proteins are more selective, often only allowing one particular type of molecule to cross.

Figure 6. Facilitated diffusion. Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

Active Transport

Active transport requires cellular energy (ATP) to move molecules from a region of low concentration to a region of high concentration, against their concentration gradient. Specialised protein carriers transport amino acids, sugars, and ions by active transport through the plasma membrane.

Osmosis and Tonicity

Osmosis is another type of passive transport in which water moves across the plasma membrane in response to differences in the concentration of solutes (or dissolved substances). It is a critical aspect of maintaining homeostasis.

In the figure below, the solute concentration is initially higher on the right hand side of the membrane. To equalise the concentration of solutes on each side of the membrane, water moves through the membrane from the left to the right hand side.

Figure 7. Osmosis. Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

 

Because water is a highly polar molecule, it is repelled by the (nonpolar) lipid core of the plasma membrane. But it passes easily through special pores called aquaporins created by proteins in the membrane.

Through osmosis, water moves from regions of low solute concentration to regions of high solute concentration. This difference in solute concentration is referred to as osmotic pressure.

  • The higher the solute concentration, the greater the osmotic pressure, and the greater the tendency of water to move into the solution.

Many molecules, particularly proteins and some ions, are prevented from diffusing across the plasma membrane. Therefore, any change in their concentration on one side of the plasma membrane forces water to move from one side of the membrane to the other.

In turn, this movement of water causes cells to lose or gain water. As a result, the size and shape of cells will change.

Tonicity

The ability of a solution to change the size and shape of cells by altering the amount of water they contain is called tonicity.

  • Isotonic solutions have the same solute and water concentrations as cells do. The interstitial fluid that surrounds our cells is an isotonic solution. Because the solute concentration is the same inside vs outside the cells, water moves in and out of the cell at the same rate. Consequently, the cell maintains its size and shape when placed in an isotonic solution (see figure below).
  • A hypertonic solution is a solution that contains more solutes (and less water) than are inside the cells. Such solutions draw water out of cells toward the higher solute concentration. Consequently, the cell shrinks when placed in a hypertonic solution (see figure below).
  • A hypotonic solution is a solution that contains fewer solutes (and more water) than are inside the cells. Such solutions draw water into cells toward the higher solute concentration. Consequently, the cell swells (and may rupture or split apart) when placed in a hypotonic solution (see figure below).

Figure 8. Concentrations of solutions. Sourced from Anatomy and Physiology 2e. J. Gordon Betts, Kelly A. Young, James A. Wise, Eddie Johnson, Brandon Poe, Dean H. Kruse, Oksana Korol, Jody E. Johnson, Mark Womble, Peter DeSaix. Apr 20, 2022. OpenStax. Houston, Texas.

 

Hypertonic solutions are sometimes injected into patients who have oedema (swelling due to fluid retention). Such solutions draw excess fluid out of the tissue spaces into the bloodstream, where it can be transported to the kidneys to be eliminated in urine.

Hypotonic solutions are sometimes injected into patients who are extremely dehydrated. But this must be done slowly to avoid rupturing the cells.

Concept Check

Question: When a red blood cell is transferred from an isotonic solution into a solution with a higher solute concentration, water will move by osmosis across the plasma membrane. What will happen to the cell?

  1. It will gain water and swell
  2. It will lose water and shrink
  3. Its volume will remain unchanged
Answer

Correct: B — It will lose water and shrink. A solution with higher solute concentration is hypertonic. Water moves via osmosis from areas of lower solute concentration to higher, causing water to flow out of the cell and the cell to shrivel (crenation).

Principles of health, safety and environment (HSE), human ethics, and academic integrity

  • Define academic integrity and identify examples of breaches in academic integrity in the context of human physiology research and teaching.

To investigate these physiology in humans, we must also protect participants and protect ourselves in the laboratory. Professional practice in physiology is therefore grounded in two domains: basic health, safety and environment procedures for laboratory work, and ethical principles for human research. 

Laboratory-based safety procedures 
At QUT, all laboratory classes and projects operate under the university’s Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) framework. Students and staff are required to: 

  • Personal protective equipment (PPE): Wear closed footwear, lab coats, safety glasses, and gloves as directed. 
  • Hygiene and contamination control: Wash hands before and after laboratory sessions, disinfect benches, avoid eating or drinking, and dispose of sharps/biological waste in designated containers. 
  • Chemical safety: Reading the safety information for all chemicals, label solutions clearly, and handle hazardous chemicals (e.g. acids, stains, fixatives) in fume hoods where required. 
  • Biological safety: Treat all human samples (saliva, blood from finger prick, tissue cultures) as potentially infectious. Follow laboratory requirements, including correct waste disposal. 
  • Equipment use: Receive induction before using equipment.
  • Emergency procedures: Know the location of eyewash stations, safety showers, spill kits, fire extinguishers, and evacuation routes. Report all incidents, even minor ones to your supervisor/ instructor.
     

 These procedures are designed to protect students, staff, and participants, and to ensure that laboratory classes provide authentic experiences without unnecessary risk.

 

Human Ethics

At QUT, all student and staff research involving humans must follow the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research. This requires proportionate ethics review and is guided by four core values:

  • Respect:  Researchers must recognize the intrinsic value of human beings, respecting their capacity to make their own decisions and abiding by the other three values.
  • Justice :  The research must ensure fair recruitment, fair distribution of benefits and burdens, and no exploitation of participants.
  • Beneficence: The benefits outweigh risks, with active monitoring to minimise harm or discomfort to participants.
  • Research merit and integrity: The research must be justifiable by its potential benefit and designed using appropriate methods, while being conducted honestly and searching for knowledge and understanding.

 

Academic Integrity

Queensland University of Technology (QUT) upholds a strict policy on academic integrity, requiring students to maintain honesty, fairness, respect, and accountability in all academic work and assessments.

Common breaches of academic integrity:

QUT identifies several key types of academic misconduct:

  • Plagiarism: Using another person's work, ideas, or expressions without proper acknowledgement. This includes direct copying, paraphrasing, or summarising without appropriate citation.

  • Self-Plagiarism: Reusing your own previously submitted work for another assessment without permission from the unit coordinator.

  • Cheating: Gaining an unfair advantage in assessments. This can include using unauthorised materials during an exam, unauthorised communication with other students, or copying from another student's work.

  • Collusion: Unauthorised collaboration with others on an assessment item. This includes working together on an individual assignment or sharing completed answers.

  • Contract Cheating: Engaging a third party to complete your assessment and submitting it as your own work. This includes using commercial essay-writing services or having a friend or family member complete the work for you.

  • Fabrication or Falsification: Inventing or altering data, information, or citations in academic work.

  • Misrepresentation: Falsely representing the originality, ownership, or engagement with an assessment task.
