Refractory periods: The bands beneath the graph indicate approximate absolute (red) and relative (amber) refractory periods. During the absolute refractory period, another action potential cannot be generated. During the relative refractory period, a stronger-than-normal stimulus may generate another action potential. Their boundaries are schematic and depend on Na⁺ channel recovery and K⁺ conductance.

What happens at each stage?

At rest, the neuronal membrane is polarised, with a typical resting membrane potential of −70 mV. The extracellular fluid contains more Na⁺, while the cytoplasm contains more K⁺. The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ at rest.

When the membrane reaches the threshold of approximately −55 mV, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ moves rapidly into the neuron. The membrane potential rises towards +30 mV. Na⁺ channels then inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels allow K⁺ to leave the neuron, causing repolarisation. Continued K⁺ movement produces a brief hyperpolarisation before the membrane potential returns towards its resting level.

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump: This transport protein uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ ions out of the neuron and 2 K⁺ ions in. It maintains the ion concentration gradients required for action potentials; it does not directly cause the rapid repolarisation phase.

Explore the sodium–potassium pump (optional animation)

The sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) uses energy from ATP to transport 3 Na⁺ ions out of the neuron and 2 K⁺ ions in. It operates continuously, maintaining the concentration gradients required for action potentials. It does not cause the rapid repolarisation phase.
